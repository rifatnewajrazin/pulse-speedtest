Fast.com is lower when…

Your ISP is congested toward Netflix, the Netflix box near you is busy, or Wi-Fi is weak. It reflects what streaming really gets.

Ookla is higher when…

A big local server exists (ISPs often host them, so traffic stays on their fast network). It shows line capacity, not everyday experience.

Which should you trust?

Use Ookla to check you get the plan you pay for. Use Fast.com to know how streaming will feel. A big gap between them can hint at ISP throttling of video.

Glossary — the confusing bits

Megabit (Mb) vs Megabyte (MB)

A bit is the smallest unit of data. A byte is 8 bits. Internet providers advertise speed in megabits per second (Mbps) because the number looks bigger. Your browser and download managers show megabytes per second (MB/s). Small b = bit, capital B = byte.

Mega, Giga — how big?

1 Gigabit = 1,000 Megabits. 1 Gigabyte = 1,000 Megabytes = 8,000 Megabits. So a 1 Gbps line downloads at most 125 MB/s.

Download vs upload

Download = getting data to you (streaming, browsing, game updates). Upload = sending data out (video calls, cloud backups, posting videos). Most home plans have much slower upload.

Ping / latency

How long a tiny message takes to reach a server and come back, in milliseconds (ms). Lower is better. Under 30 ms is excellent, under 50 ms is great for gaming, 50–100 ms is playable, over 150 ms feels laggy.

Jitter

How much your ping bounces around. Low jitter = smooth calls and games. High jitter = choppy voice, rubber-banding in games.

Packet loss and dropped pings

Data travels in small packets. If some never arrive they must be re-sent, causing stutter. Anything above 1% is worth fixing; above 2.5% hurts calls and games a lot. Weak Wi-Fi is the usual cause. A browser can't count lost packets directly (they are re-sent behind the scenes), so Pulse shows dropped pings: pings with no reply within 2 seconds. That catches dropouts and heavy loss, but light loss only shows up as higher ping and jitter.

Bufferbloat

When your connection is busy, ping can shoot up because packets queue behind each other. Ping during download or upload much higher than idle ping means one person's download makes everyone else lag.

Real, shared and CGNAT IPs

Your public IP is your address on the internet. Many providers put several customers behind the same public address (CGNAT) — that's a shared IP: fine for browsing, but a problem for hosting servers, port forwarding and some games. A website can only spot it sometimes (for example when two servers see two different addresses from you). The sure check: open your router's admin page and compare its WAN IP with the IP shown here. If they differ, or the WAN IP starts with 100.64–100.127, 10. or 192.168., your IP is shared. A VPN or proxy replaces your real IP with the VPN server's one.

Why is my speed lower than my plan?

Wi-Fi (walls, distance, 2.4 GHz), many devices at once, an old router, or ISP congestion at busy hours. Try again over an Ethernet cable and compare — that shows your real line speed without Wi-Fi in the way.
